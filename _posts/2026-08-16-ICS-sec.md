Industrial control systems were never designed with cybersecurity in mind. For decades, that was fine. A PLC sitting on a closed network, talking Modbus to a handful of sensors, had no meaningful attack surface. Physical isolation did the job that firewalls do in IT environments.

That era is over.

The push toward predictive maintenance, remote supervision, and ERP integration has quietly connected OT networks to the rest of the world. The air gap is gone, or at best, porous. And the threat actors who used to target enterprise IT have noticed.

This article is the first in a series documenting a project I conducted at OCP Group's Daoui Washing Plant in Khouribga, where I built and tested a behavioral security monitoring architecture for an OT environment. Before getting into the technical setup, I want to establish why this kind of project matters, what the actual threat looks like, and why the tools most organizations already have are not enough.

## The IT/OT Convergence Problem

In a traditional OT setup, the priorities are uptime, determinism, and safety. A SCADA system controlling a phosphate washing process cannot be rebooted for a patch. A PLC cannot afford a 200ms latency spike because an antivirus decided to scan something. The people who built these systems optimized for availability, not confidentiality.

IT security works the other way around. Confidentiality comes first. Patching is expected. Reboots happen.

When you connect these two worlds, you inherit the attack surface of IT while keeping the operational constraints of OT. That is the core tension. You cannot apply enterprise security practices directly to industrial environments without risking the very uptime you are trying to protect.

## What the Attack Timeline Actually Looks Like

The industrial security incidents that changed how the industry thinks about OT threats did not happen in isolation. They followed a clear progression.

![ICS Cyberattack Timeline 2010-2024](/assets/img/posts/wazuh_project/Napkin2.png)
*Figure 2 : Major cyberattacks targeting industrial systems from Stuxnet to Pipedream. The frequency and sophistication have only increased.*

**Stuxnet (2010)** is the reference case. It entered an air-gapped Iranian nuclear facility via a USB drive, exploited four Windows zero-days simultaneously, and physically destroyed roughly 1,000 uranium enrichment centrifuges by subtly manipulating Siemens S7 PLC parameters, while displaying normal readings to operators. The damage was physical. The vector was digital. And the target had no internet connection.

**WannaCry (2017)** arrived seven years later and hit differently. It was not targeted. It was opportunistic. It exploited EternalBlue, an NSA-developed SMB vulnerability leaked by Shadow Brokers, and propagated automatically without any user interaction. In 72 hours, it infected 200,000 machines across 150 countries. Manufacturing plants, hospitals, logistics companies. The NHS in the UK ground to a halt. Renault factories stopped production. The economic damage is estimated between 4 and 8 billion dollars.

What made WannaCry particularly damaging in OT environments is a detail that gets overlooked: 98% of the infected machines were running Windows 7. In enterprise IT, Windows 7 was already considered legacy. In OT environments, it is still common today, because updating a production system means stopping it, and stopping it has a cost.

![Stuxnet vs WannaCry Comparison](/assets/img/posts/wazuh_project/napkin4.png)
*Figure 3 : Stuxnet and WannaCry targeted different things in different ways, but both exploited the same structural weakness: OT environments that were not built to defend against cyberattacks.*

## The Polymorphic Threat: Why Signature Detection Fails

Most endpoint security tools work by comparing files against a database of known malicious signatures, hashes, patterns. It is effective against known threats. The problem is that modern malware does not stay still long enough to be catalogued.

A polymorphic malware embeds a mutation engine. Every time it replicates, it generates a new encryption key, re-encrypts its payload, and produces a new decryptor stub. The resulting binary has a completely different hash than the previous instance. The behavior is identical. The signature is not.

![Polymorphic Malware Mutation Mechanism](/assets/img/posts/wazuh_project/napkin3.png)
*Figure 4 : A polymorphic malware generates a unique binary signature on every copy, while executing the exact same malicious behavior. Signature-based detection is structurally blind to this.*

IBM's X-Force Threat Intelligence Index reports that 31% of malware observed in enterprise environments uses polymorphic evasion techniques. The number is likely higher in OT, where antivirus databases are updated less frequently and endpoint agents are often not deployed at all.

The four categories of evasive malware are worth distinguishing:

| Type | Signature | Code Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static | Fixed | Fixed | Legacy viruses |
| Oligomorphic | Few variants | Fixed | Early 2000s worms |
| Polymorphic | Unique per copy | Fixed | Emotet, Zeus, CryptoLocker |
| Metamorphic | Unique per copy | Fully rewritten | Simile, state-level APTs |

The key column is the third one. The code structure, and by extension the behavior, stays fixed. What changes is the packaging. This is exactly why behavioral detection works where signature detection fails: it does not care what the malware looks like. It watches what it does.

## What Behavioral Detection Actually Means

Instead of matching a file hash against a database, behavioral detection monitors 
runtime actions. When a process reads a file, encrypts its content with AES, writes 
a new file with a `.WNCRY` extension and deletes the original, that sequence is 
detectable regardless of what the process looks like. When `vssadmin.exe` gets called 
with `delete shadows /all`, that is a known ransomware behavior pattern, independently 
of which binary triggered it. Sysmon captures these actions at the kernel level via 
Event ID 11 (FileCreate) and Event ID 23 (FileDelete), giving Wazuh exactly what it 
needs to correlate and alert without ever seeing the malicious binary itself.
